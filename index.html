Mapping a Biogeochemical Cycle

When
Friday, 10/23, in class
Duration
50 minutes
Group size
Varies. Work with the students sitting around you.
Tools
Tutorial: Dr. Ted Pavlic’s Drawing Causal Loop Diagrams in Insight Maker. Or, the dedicated Cycle Systems Builder on the last slide of this deck.

Objective. Describe a major biogeochemical cycle and analyze its interactions with human activities to evaluate its role in supporting Earth’s life-supporting systems. Final deliverable: a single, cohesive visual illustration (created collaboratively via Zoom Whiteboard, InsightMaker, Miro, Canva, PowerPoint, or a similar platform) divided into three clear sections: Title, Diagram, and Summary.

First

Pick one cycle: The Nitrogen Cycle · The Phosphorus Cycle · The Carbon Cycle · The Water Cycle · The Oxygen Cycle

Context: ground your diagram in a specific geographic context. You may base it on the local Sonoran Desert (Phoenix area), or select a comparable ecosystem (e.g., tropical forest, temperate forest, savanna) if it provides a more compelling case for your cycle.

The three sections of your illustration

1.Top Section: Title

A clear, descriptive title that names your cycle and your chosen ecosystem.

e.g., “The Nitrogen Cycle in the Sonoran Desert”

2.Middle Section: The Main Illustration

Map the complete cycle by explicitly annotating the pools, inflows, and outflows. Use boxes or circles for stocks, labeled arrows for flows, and icons or clipart to highlight human touchpoints.

Key components (stocks / pools): the main reservoirs where the element resides (e.g., atmosphere, soil organic matter, vegetation, microbes, water bodies).

Key processes (flows): the essential biological, chemical, or physical processes that move the element between pools (e.g., photosynthesis, nitrogen fixation, nitrification, decomposition, erosion, evaporation).

Human impacts: integrate 2–3 human-caused disruptions to the natural cycle. A Nitrogen team might illustrate fertilizer runoff, fossil fuel combustion, or soil crust degradation; a Carbon team might focus on vehicle emissions, deforestation, or urban concrete replacing biomass storage.

3.Bottom Section: Summary & Identity

Synthesis statement: a brief, original summary analyzing the cycle’s unique dynamics within your chosen ecosystem.

“In the arid Phoenix desert, natural nitrogen cycling is highly limited by moisture, making biological soil crusts crucial for nitrogen fixation; however, urban fossil fuel combustion heavily amplifies local deposition…”

Submission

Collaboration: work actively with the students around you. Individual submission: take a high-resolution screenshot or export your final work as a PDF. Every student must upload their own copy to the Canvas link to receive credit.

What your diagram predicts

A qualitative sketch, not a measurement. Every pool starts at 100% of its own size, each process runs at the speed you gave it, scaled by how full its source is, and a + or − arrow into a process speeds it up or slows it down. Solid lines include your human impacts; dashed lines leave them out.

How this works

The builder makes the single illustration the assignment asks for, in its three sections: the Title band across the top, the Diagram on the canvas, and the Summary you write in its own window. Save as PDF prints all three on one sheet.

The four kinds of piece
Stock A pool or reservoir where the element sits: the atmosphere, soil organic matter, an aquifer.
Flow A process that moves the element from one pool to another: fixation, decomposition, runoff. A cloud at either end means the material comes from, or goes to, somewhere outside your system.
Condition Something that speeds or slows a process without being a pool itself: rainfall, temperature, microbial activity. These are the variables of a causal loop diagram.
Human impact A human touchpoint, drawn with its icon. A flow can also be marked human-caused in its editor, as fossil fuel combustion would be.
Drawing

⇒ Flow draws a whole process in one drag. From one stock to another, it adds the valve and both pipes. From a stock into empty space, the material leaves the system. From empty space onto a stock, it enters from outside. You can also drop a process from the palette and pipe it in afterwards.

+ Arrow and − Arrow draw causal links. A + means the two move the same way (more rainfall, more leaching); a − means they move in opposite ways. Point arrows at a process to show what speeds or slows it. Clicking an arrow flips its sign.

Double-click any piece to rename it (suggestions for your cycle appear as you type), mark a flow human-caused, or explain a human impact. Delete removes a selected piece, and Undo takes back the last change.

Feedback and the model

The panel names every feedback loop it finds. Pipes count: an inflow raises its stock and an outflow lowers it, so an arrow from a stock to its own outflow closes a balancing loop. ▶ Run the model plays the diagram forward and compares each pool with and without your human impacts. It is a sketch for thinking with, not a measurement.

Saving your work

Everything saves in this browser as you go, so a refresh loses nothing. Save downloads a .cycle.json file you can Open later on any computer, or send to a groupmate. Save as PDF makes the file you upload to Canvas; every student uploads their own copy.
